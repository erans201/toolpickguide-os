# Client Onboarding Checklist (2026): 10 Steps From Signed Deal to First Deliverable

**A client onboarding checklist** is what turns "they said yes" into "they're glad they did." The first two weeks after a signature set the tone for the whole relationship.

Here's the uncomfortable part. Most onboarding problems aren't bad work; they're forgotten steps. The deposit nobody chased, the login nobody asked for, the kickoff call with no agenda.

This guide gives you 10 steps in order, a copy-ready checklist, a welcome email and versions for five kinds of service businesses. It covers onboarding one new client of a service business, not employee onboarding, software user onboarding or bank identity checks.

**Quick answer: the 10-step client onboarding checklist**

1. Send the contract and collect the deposit
2. Send the intake form
3. Send the welcome email
4. Set the client up in your system
5. Book and run the kickoff call
6. Collect access and permissions
7. Open the client portal or shared folder
8. Share the timeline and communication rules
9. Deliver the first deliverable (a quick win)
10. Run the first check-in

Copy the checklist below into your project tool and reuse it for every new client.

## The client onboarding checklist: 10 steps in order

Think of onboarding as the check-in desk of a hotel. Guests forgive a small room; they don't forgive a lost reservation. These client onboarding steps are the reservation system.

### 1. Send the contract and collect the deposit

Nothing starts before both are done. Not the kickoff, not "a quick look at your site," nothing. Work before payment trains clients to treat your deadlines as optional.

E-signatures make this fast. Under the US [ESIGN Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-106publ229), a signature or contract can't be denied legal effect just because it's electronic. Cornell's legal dictionary has a plain explanation of what counts as an [e-signature](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/electronic_signature), and our guide to [e-signature tools](/best-doc-signing-tools/) compares the options.

Send the contract and the [DEPOSIT] invoice together, in one message, so the client can finish both in one sitting.

If the deposit hasn't arrived after a couple of days, send one friendly nudge with the invoice link again. Most late deposits are a busy inbox, not a change of heart.

**Done when:** the signed contract is filed and the [DEPOSIT] payment has cleared.

### 2. Send the intake form

The intake form collects the facts once, before the kickoff. Contact details, goals, deadlines, brand assets, past attempts, who approves what.

Asking upfront means the kickoff call is spent on decisions, not on spelling the client's company name. Our [client intake form template](/client-intake-form-template/) has the questions ready to copy.

Keep the form short enough to finish in one go. Ask only what you'll actually use in the next month; everything else can wait for the first check-in.

**Done when:** the client has submitted the form and you've read every answer.

### 3. Send the welcome email

The welcome email answers the three questions every new client has: who's who, what happens next and how to reach you. Send it the same day the contract is signed.

Keep it short and friendly. A client who knows the next three steps doesn't need to email you asking for them. Use the template further down.

This is also the moment buyer's remorse creeps in. A warm, specific email right after the signature tells the client they picked someone organized.

**Done when:** the email is sent and the kickoff date is in the client's calendar.

### 4. Set the client up in your system

Create the client record, the project, the shared folder and, if they're on a retainer, the recurring invoice. Do it now, while the details are fresh.

This is where [client management software](/crm-operations-best-client-management-software/) earns its keep. One record holds the contract, the intake answers, the invoices and every message.

Use the same naming pattern for every client: company name first, then the project. Six months from now, you'll find things in seconds instead of minutes.

**Done when:** the client, project, folder and billing schedule all exist and link to each other.

### 5. Book and run the kickoff call

Run every kickoff on a fixed agenda: goals, how success will be measured, roles on both sides, the timeline and open questions. The same five items, every time.

Send the notes within one business day. Written notes turn "I thought we agreed..." into "here's what we agreed."

Invite the person who signs off on the work, not just your day-to-day contact. Approvals that skip the kickoff tend to come back as surprises in week three.

**Done when:** the call has happened and the notes are in the client's inbox.

### 6. Collect access and permissions

Make a list of every login you need: website, ad accounts, analytics, shared drives, software. Then ask for all of it at once, in one request.

Ask the client to invite you as a user instead of sending passwords. Invites can be revoked later; a password in an email thread lives forever.

Keep a simple access log: what you have, who granted it and when. When the project ends, that list becomes your offboarding checklist.

**Done when:** every item on the access list works from your side.

### 7. Open the client portal or shared folder

Give the client one place for files and messages, so nothing important lives in email threads. A client portal or a simple shared folder both work.

Name it something obvious, like "[CLIENT NAME] · Project hub." Then send the link with one sentence about what goes where.

**Done when:** the client has opened it and found the first file you put there.

### 8. Share the timeline and communication rules

Write down the milestones, the response time you commit to ([X business days]), the meeting rhythm and who approves what. One page is enough.

Rules feel stiff, but they prevent the slow drift where every message feels urgent. Clients relax when they know when they'll hear from you.

Say what counts as a change in scope, too. A clear line now saves an awkward money conversation later.

**Done when:** the client has the one-page timeline and has said it works for them.

### 9. Deliver the first deliverable (a quick win)

Ship something small and visible early: a first draft, an audit, a cleaned-up file, a short plan. It doesn't have to be big; it has to be real.

A quick win confirms the client made the right call. It also gives you an early read on how they give feedback.

**Done when:** the client has the first deliverable and has replied to it.

### 10. Run the first check-in

After the first deliverable, run a short call or send an email. Ask two things: what's working, and what should change?

Small fixes now are cheap. The same fixes after two months of quiet frustration are expensive.

End the check-in by confirming the next milestone. Onboarding is over; the regular rhythm takes over from here.

**Done when:** you've logged the answers and made any changes you agreed to.

## Client onboarding steps by business type

The 10 steps work everywhere. Each type of business adds a few items of its own.

### Marketing agencies

Agencies live and die by access. Get ad accounts, analytics and brand assets in week one, or the first report goes out empty.

Agree on the reporting rhythm at kickoff. If you're also choosing a system to run all this, compare our [CRM for marketing agencies](/best-crm-for-marketing-agencies/) picks.

- [ ] Admin or user access to ad accounts and analytics
- [ ] Brand assets: logos, fonts, colors, past campaigns
- [ ] Reporting rhythm and report recipients agreed

### Accountants and bookkeepers

Start with an engagement letter that states the scope. Then collect prior-year returns and statements through a secure channel, not email attachments.

If you'll need to talk to the IRS about the client's account, use [Form 8821](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8821). It lets a client authorize a person or firm to inspect or receive their confidential tax information for the tax types and years listed. For safe document handling, read the [IRS guide to safeguarding taxpayer data](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4557.pdf).

Accounting practice management tools such as Karbon can run these steps for a firm; see our guide to [practice management software for accountants](/best-practice-management-accountants/). This isn't tax or legal advice.

- [ ] Engagement letter signed
- [ ] Prior-year returns and statements collected securely
- [ ] Form 8821 signed, if you need to speak with the IRS

### Law firms

Run a conflict check before you accept the matter. Then send an engagement letter that states the scope and fees.

Handle retainers carefully. A security [retainer](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/retainer) is paid up front, but it doesn't belong to the lawyer until it's earned, and it goes into a trust account. Rules vary by state, and this isn't legal advice.

- [ ] Conflict check done before accepting
- [ ] Engagement letter with scope and fees signed

### Coaches

Coaching clients buy a feeling of progress, so the welcome packet matters. Put the package and session count in writing, so nobody argues about "one more call" later.

Keep the human parts human. The kickoff call and the first check-in are where trust gets built, and no automation replaces them.

Review the checklist every few months. If a step keeps getting skipped, either it isn't needed or it needs an owner; decide which, and update the template so your client onboarding process stays honest.

## Don't mess this up: 6 onboarding mistakes that sour new clients

**Don't start work before the deposit clears.** It feels generous. It actually tells the client your schedule bends.

**Don't ask for the same information twice.** If it's in the intake form, read it before the kickoff. Asking again tells the client nobody read it.

**Don't share passwords by email.** Ask for user invites instead. They're safer, and you can remove them when the project ends.

**Don't skip the kickoff agenda.** A call without an agenda turns into a chat. A chat produces no decisions.

**Don't go quiet in week one.** Silence after a signature makes clients nervous. A short update every few days beats a perfect one next week.

**Don't wait for the client to tell you something is wrong.** The first check-in exists so you ask first. By the time a client complains, they've been unhappy for a while.

Follow this client onboarding checklist every time, even with the easy clients. Especially with the easy clients.

## FAQs About Client Onboarding

### What should a client onboarding checklist include?

At minimum: a signed contract and deposit, an intake form, a welcome email, setup in your system, a kickoff call, access, a shared space for files, a timeline, a first deliverable and a first check-in. That's the 10 steps above.

### How long should client onboarding take?

It depends on the service. A useful rule is that onboarding ends when the client has the first deliverable and you've done the first check-in, whether that takes days or weeks.

### Are electronic signatures legally valid in the US?

Under the ESIGN Act (Public Law 106-229, from 2000), a signature or contract can't be denied legal effect solely because it's electronic. Check with a lawyer for special document types; this isn't legal advice.

### What's the difference between a proposal, a contract and onboarding?

The proposal wins the deal and the contract sets the binding terms. Onboarding is everything between the signature and the first deliverable.

### Should I start work before the deposit is paid?

No. Start after the contract is signed and the deposit has cleared, so both sides are committed.

### Can I automate client onboarding?

Partly. Automate the repeated sends (contract, invoice, intake form, welcome email) with tools like HoneyBook or Dubsado, and keep the kickoff call and check-in personal.
