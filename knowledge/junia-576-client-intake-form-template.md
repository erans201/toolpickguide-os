**A good client intake form template** is the difference between a first call that moves the work forward and one where you still don't know what the client wants.

An intake call without a form is a first date where you forget to ask their name.

You end up smiling, nodding, and realizing 20 minutes later you still don't know what they actually want, what they can pay, or who is making the decision.

A client intake form fixes that. Not because forms are magical, but because you're tired of asking the same questions across 14 emails, then missing the one detail that blows up the project later.

This is for service businesses only: law firms, accountants and tax preparers, coaches and consultants, marketing agencies, real estate agents, and photographers.

If you run a healthcare practice, stop here. Medical intake has its own rules (HIPAA), and this template isn't for that.

## Intake form vs questionnaire vs contract

Three documents get mixed up all the time:

- **Intake form:** before you say yes. It tells you whether the client is a fit and what they need.
- **Questionnaire:** after they book. It collects project details, like a [photography client questionnaire](/photography-client-questionnaire/) for a shoot.
- **Contract:** the agreement itself. An intake form never replaces it.

## What a client intake form should do (and what it shouldn't)

A good intake form does three jobs.

First, it collects the basics, so you don't waste your first call playing administrative ping-pong.

Second, it exposes risk early: weird deadlines, budget mismatches, missing decision-makers, messy data.

Third, it sets expectations: scope, communication, how you work, and what you won't do.

What it shouldn't do: read like a mortgage application. If the form is longer than the service, people will either abandon it or rush through it.

## Quick setup rules (so the form actually gets filled out)

Keep it to one screen per section. Use plain words: no "organization name" if your clients say "company." Mark what's required versus nice to have.

Put a short "why we ask" on the two or three questions people resist, mostly budget and deadline. A single line like "helps us recommend the right package" can save five awkward messages later.

One more thing. If you offer multiple services, don't build one mega-form that covers every edge case. Build one core intake, then add a short add-on section based on what they select.

## The client intake form template (25 questions)

Below are 25 questions you can copy into Jotform or any other form builder. They're grouped by purpose, not by industry. Pick what fits, delete what doesn't.

### Section 1: Contact and decision basics (questions 1 to 6)

These questions stop the "who are you again?" thread before it starts.

**1) Full name**

If you serve businesses, consider separate fields for first and last name. It helps with contracts and invoicing.

**2) Preferred email + preferred phone (optional)**

Ask for the channel they actually check. Otherwise you'll send three emails to the address they never open.

**3) Company / brand name (if applicable)**

For photographers and coaches, many clients are individuals. Let them skip it.

**4) Website and/or key links**

Website, LinkedIn, Instagram, a listing profile, a portfolio, a Google Business Profile: whatever is relevant. You're trying to see what they have now, not judge it.

**5) Best way to contact you, and typical response window**

Example choices: email only, phone, text, WhatsApp, Slack, "I check email once a day."

This one prevents the classic mismatch where they expect instant replies and you run a real business.

**6) Who is the decision-maker for this project?**

Options: me, my partner, my boss, a committee, not sure yet. If it isn't them, you just saved yourself two weeks of pitching the wrong person.

### Section 2: The reason they're here (questions 7 to 11)

This is where you pull the real problem out of polite small talk.

**7) What service are you looking for?**

Use checkboxes if you offer bundles, and keep the options tight. If they pick "other," require a short description.

**8) What prompted you to reach out right now?**

This is the "why now" question. Someone got sued. Taxes are due.

Ads stopped performing. A house needs staging. Urgency changes everything, including what you should propose.

**9) What would a successful outcome look like, in plain language?**

Not "optimize brand awareness." More like "get 20 qualified leads a month," "stop getting tax notices," or "sell the house in 30 days."

If they can't describe success, they're not ready, or they need you to lead harder.

**10) What have you tried already? What happened?**

This is your shortcut to avoiding past mistakes. It also reveals whether they blame everyone else for results, which is… useful information.

**11) What's your biggest concern about hiring help for this?**

People say "price," but often they mean "I got burned before" or "I'm worried you won't understand my industry." This question surfaces the objection before it becomes a surprise.

### Section 3: Scope, logistics, and constraints (questions 12 to 17)

Scope creep is a slow leak. This section finds the holes.

**12) What exactly needs to be done? List the deliverables you expect.**

For a marketing agency, that might be "website rebuild + 6 emails + 12 social posts." For an accountant, "bookkeeping cleanup + monthly close." For a lawyer, "review this contract and redline it."

If they don't know, that's fine. Add a prompt: "If you're not sure, describe the situation and what you want to happen next."

**13) What's the timeline? Any hard deadlines?**

A hard deadline is a court date, closing date, tax filing deadline, event date, or listing launch. A soft deadline is "sometime in Q1." You need both.

**14) What's flexible: scope, timeline, or budget? Pick one.**

This is a favorite operations question because it forces reality. If they pick "nothing," at least you know you're on a unicorn hunt.

**15) What's the current status?**

Options: starting from scratch, already in progress, replacing a provider, emergency fix. If they're replacing someone, you may need transition time and access to assets.

**16) Are there any must-follow requirements?**

Think brand guidelines, legal constraints, compliance rules, platform restrictions, brokerage policies, or an internal approval process. Not asking early is how you build something they aren't allowed to use.

**17) Who else needs to be involved, and what's the approval process?**

If you need sign-off from a spouse, CFO, broker, or board, get it on record. Projects don't stall because of work. They stall because of approvals.

### Section 4: Budget and commercial fit (questions 18 to 20)

Yes, budget questions feel awkward. So does writing a proposal they can't afford. It's like tailoring a suit without asking the size.

**18) What budget range have you set aside for this?**

Offer ranges that match your pricing, not random numbers, for example "under 1k, 1k to 3k, 3k to 7k, 7k+" in your currency, or monthly retainer ranges. If you do custom work, add: "If you're not sure, pick your best guess."

**19) How do you prefer to pay?**

Options: card, bank transfer, invoice, payment plan, retainer up front. Law and accounting firms often have specific billing rules, and agencies too, so get ahead of it.

**20) If we recommend something outside your current budget, do you want alternatives?**

This keeps the conversation productive. Some clients want a smaller-scope option. Others want "tell me the real plan, I'll find the money."

### Section 5: Files, access, and source of truth (questions 21 to 23)

This section saves you from the nightmare where everything is "in someone's inbox."

**21) What documents, files, or assets already exist, and where are they stored?**

Examples: prior tax returns, contracts, a brand kit, a photo library, listing copy, CRM exports, financial statements. Also ask where they live: a shared drive, email, or "on my laptop."

**22) Who owns the accounts and logins we'll need?**

This matters for marketing platforms, domains, bookkeeping software, even shared calendars. If they don't own it, you might not get access when you need it.

**23) Who will be our main day-to-day contact?**

It isn't always the decision-maker. You need the person who answers questions and can move things forward.

### Section 6: Preferences and boundaries (questions 24 to 25)

Most service headaches are expectation headaches. This section is the aspirin.

**24) Communication preferences: meeting frequency, call length, and updates**

Give options like weekly 30 minutes, biweekly 45 minutes, async updates only, or "only meet when decisions are needed." This stops the "can we hop on a quick call?" death by a thousand cuts.

**25) Anything else we should know to do great work for you?**

Leave this open-ended. People tell you the weird but important stuff here: "I travel constantly," "I hate phone calls," "we have a strict brand voice," "talk to my assistant."

## Add-on question sets by industry

Don't bloat your core form. Use conditional logic: if they select "tax prep," show tax questions; if "photo shoot," show shoot questions.

### Law firms

You'll have your own ethics and conflict-check workflow. Lawyers must make reasonable efforts to protect client information under [ABA Model Rule 1.6](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_6_confidentiality_of_information/), so collect only what you need before engagement. For a full intake system, see our guide to [law firm intake](/best-crm-for-law-firms/).

- What is the matter type? (contract review, business formation, employment issue, litigation support)
- Are there upcoming dates? (court, hearing, filing, negotiation)
- Names of all other parties involved (for conflict checks)
- What documents should we review first?
- What's the desired outcome? (settle, enforce, avoid risk, negotiate better terms)

### Accountants and tax preparers

This is where "quick question" clients turn into "why is this a mess" clients. Ask early, and ask only for what you need.

Never collect full Social Security numbers on an open web form. The IRS's [Publication 4557](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4557.pdf) covers safeguarding taxpayer data.

- What entities are involved? (individual, LLC, S corp, C corp, partnership)
- Which year(s) are we working on?
- Is your bookkeeping up to date? If not, how far behind?
- Any notices from the IRS or state agencies?
- Which accounting software do you use?

### Coaches and consultants

The risk here is mismatched expectations. They want transformation; you offer a process.

- What does support look like to you? (calls, async, templates, accountability)
- What's your biggest obstacle right now?
- Have you worked with a coach or consultant before? What worked, what didn't?
- Time you can realistically commit per week
- Any non-negotiables (boundaries, topics, schedule)

### Marketing agencies

Marketing intake fails when you don't have access, or don't have a goal you can measure.

- Primary goal (leads, revenue, pipeline, retention, launch)
- Target audience in one sentence
- Current channels (SEO, ads, email, social, referrals)
- What tracking exists? (GA4, CRM, pixels, call tracking)
- Who approves work, and how fast?

### Real estate agents

Real estate is timelines plus emotions, plus paperwork. Get the basics tight.

- Buyer or seller (or both)
- Location(s) and must-have criteria
- Timeline and hard constraints (lease end, school year, job relocation)
- Financing status (cash, pre-approved, not started)
- Communication preference (text or call) and availability for showings

### Photographers

Photography is a logistics business wearing a creative hat. Keep intake short, then send a [photography client questionnaire](/photography-client-questionnaire/) after booking.

- Shoot type (family, brand, headshots, event, real estate, wedding)
- Date and location(s)
- Usage needs (personal, commercial, paid ads, print)
- Style references (links)
- Must-have shots (if any)

## How to send your intake form (and what to do with the answers)

Put the form right after someone raises their hand, before the deep call. If you wait until after the call, you'll still have the same call. Just more tired.

A simple flow that works for most service businesses:

1. A lead reaches out.
2. An auto-reply sends the link: "Fill this out so we can prep and use our time well."
3. You review, then book the call or send next steps.

**Online form, PDF, or CRM?** An online form is easiest to complete on a phone, and the answers land in one place. PDFs get printed, scanned, and lost.

For a concrete example: Jotform's free plan includes 5 active forms and 100 monthly submissions, and its Bronze plan is $34/month billed annually ($39 monthly). Compare more options in our guide to [form builders](/best-form-builders-for-lead-capture/).

**Make the answers flow into the client record, not your inbox.** Send a "got it" confirmation with next steps, and route the answers to your CRM so the right person sees them. Once they sign, the intake answers feed your [client onboarding software](/client-onboarding-software/).

## Keeping intake data safe

Intake forms collect personal information, so treat it that way. The [FTC's data security guide for business](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business) boils it down to five principles:

- **Take stock:** know which intake forms you run and where the answers are stored.
- **Scale down:** ask only for what you need to evaluate the work.
- **Lock it:** limit who can see submissions, and use strong passwords and two-factor login.
- **Pitch it:** delete intake data from leads who never became clients, on a schedule.
- **Plan ahead:** know what you'd do if a form account were ever compromised.

## A few "don't mess this up" intake form tips

**Don't ask 60 questions up front.**
Long forms get abandoned or rushed. Keep the core form short and use add-on sections.

**Don't collect what you don't need.**
Every extra field is a privacy risk. Sensitive details can wait until after engagement.

**Don't let answers die in email.**
Route submissions to your CRM or project system. An answer nobody reads is a question you'll ask again.

**Don't skip "how did you hear about us?"**
It's the cheapest marketing report you'll ever get. Add it as an optional question.

**Don't wait a week to follow up.**
Reply within a business day. The fastest response often wins the client.

## The bottom line

Your best client intake form template isn't the longest one. It's the one that stops the repeated emails and catches the landmines early.

Copy it into your tool, run it for two weeks, then adjust based on what you keep chasing.

## FAQs (Frequently Asked Questions)

### What should a client intake form include?
Contact details, the decision-maker, the service needed and why now, what success looks like, scope and deadlines, a budget range, existing files and access, and communication preferences. The 25-question client intake form template above covers all of them in six sections.

### How long should an intake form be?
Long enough to qualify the client, short enough to finish in a few minutes. Keep the core form to about 25 questions, mark the optional ones, and move industry-specific questions into add-on sections.

### Is a client intake form the same as a contract?
No. An intake form collects information before you agree to work together.

A contract is the binding agreement. Use the intake answers to write your proposal, then send a separate contract.

### Can I use a free form builder?
Yes. Many form builders have free plans.

Jotform's free plan, for example, includes 5 active forms and 100 monthly submissions. Check the submission limits and where the data is stored before you rely on a free plan.

### How do I store client intake information securely?
Limit who can see submissions, use two-factor login, collect only what you need, and delete data from leads who never became clients. The FTC's five principles (take stock, scale down, lock it, pitch it, plan ahead) are a good checklist.
