# Service Contract Template (2026): 14 Clauses Every Service Business Needs

**A service contract template** is the cheapest insurance a service business can buy. You write it once, fix it as you learn, and reuse it with every client who says yes.

Without one, the same four fights keep coming back. The project that grows a little every week. The client who pays "next Friday" for a month.

The cancellation the week before the start date. And the awkward email about who actually owns the files.

This guide gives you the 14 clauses a solid service agreement needs, a fill-in-the-blank template you can copy, tips for adapting it to your kind of business, and the simplest way to get it signed online.

One scope note: this is about agreements between a service business and its clients. It's not about employment contracts, leases, or the "extended service contracts" sold with cars and appliances.

> **Legal note:** This is general information, not legal advice. Laws differ by state and industry, so have a lawyer review your contract before you rely on it.

## Quick answer: what a service contract template needs

A usable service contract template covers these 14 clauses, in roughly this order:

1. **Parties and effective date:** who is agreeing, and from when.
2. **Scope of services:** exactly what you will do.
3. **Deliverables and acceptance:** what the client receives and how "done" is decided.
4. **Timeline and milestones:** when each piece happens.
5. **Fees and payment terms:** what it costs and when invoices are due.
6. **Deposit or retainer:** what is paid up front, and whether it comes back.
7. **Late payments and expenses:** what happens when money is late, and who pays costs.
8. **Revisions and change requests:** how many rounds, and how extra work is priced.
9. **Client responsibilities:** what the client must provide, and by when.
10. **Cancellation and rescheduling:** how either side can move or cancel.
11. **Intellectual property and ownership:** who owns the work, and when.
12. **Confidentiality:** what stays private.
13. **Limitation of liability and indemnity:** how risk is capped and shared.
14. **Termination and dispute resolution:** how the agreement ends and how fights get settled.

## What a service contract is (and what it isn't)

A contract is an agreement between parties that creates mutual obligations enforceable by law. The [basic elements of a contract](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/contract) are mutual assent (an offer and an acceptance), consideration, capacity and legality.

That word [consideration](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/consideration) sounds fancy, but the idea is simple. Both sides take on an obligation: you promise the work, the client promises to pay. A "contract" where only one side promises anything is just a wish list.

Three documents often get mixed up, so here is the plain-English version:

- **Proposal:** the pitch before the client says yes. It sells the idea and the price.
- **Service contract:** the binding terms after the client says yes. It sets the rules of the relationship.
- **Statement of work (SOW):** the project details for one job. Many businesses sign one master service contract and then add a short SOW for each new project.

If you work with repeat clients, that last setup saves a lot of paperwork. The legal terms stay put, and only the project page changes.

## The 14 clauses every service contract template needs

Think of the clauses below as the walls of a house. Skip one, and the weather gets in exactly where you didn't build.

### 1. Parties and effective date

This clause names who is agreeing. Use full legal business names, the type of entity where it applies, addresses, and the person signing for each side.

Add the effective date, which can differ from the signing date. **Mistake to avoid:** writing a client's brand name instead of the legal entity that will pay your invoices.

### 2. Scope of services

The scope clause says what you will do, in concrete terms. "Social media management" is a mood. "Twelve posts per month on two platforms, written and scheduled by us" is a scope.

It also helps to say what is **not** included. A contract without a clear scope is a buffet with no closing time. **Mistake to avoid:** vague verbs like "support," "assist" or "manage" with no numbers or limits.

### 3. Deliverables and acceptance

List what the client actually receives: files, reports, sessions, a live website. Then define how a deliverable counts as accepted.

A common approach is a review window: the client approves or sends specific feedback within [NUMBER] days, or the deliverable is treated as accepted. **Mistake to avoid:** no acceptance rule, so a project can sit "almost done" forever.

### 4. Timeline and milestones

Set the start date, the key milestones and the end date or ongoing term. Tie your deadlines to the client's inputs, so a late brief moves your dates too.

**Mistake to avoid:** promising fixed dates while the client controls half of what you need to hit them.

### 5. Fees and payment terms

State the fee structure: flat project fee, hourly rate, monthly retainer or package. Then say when invoices go out, when they are due, and how the client can pay.

Write it so a stranger could calculate the invoice. **Mistake to avoid:** "fees to be agreed" anywhere in a signed contract.

### 6. Deposit or retainer

A deposit protects the time you block off for a client. Say how much is due before work starts (the template uses [DEPOSIT AMOUNT]), when it's due, and whether it is refundable.

If it's non-refundable, say so plainly and explain what it covers, such as reserved dates or early planning work. **Mistake to avoid:** calling it "refundable" in an email and "non-refundable" in the contract.

### 7. Late payments and expenses

Spell out what happens when an invoice is late: a reminder schedule, a pause in work after [NUMBER] days, and any late fee you're allowed to charge where you work. Late-fee rules vary by state, so check yours.

Also cover expenses: travel, stock photos, software, printing. Say whether they're billed at cost and whether they need approval first. **Mistake to avoid:** no pause-work right, so you keep working for a client who has stopped paying.

### 8. Revisions and change requests

Say how many revision rounds are included and what counts as a revision versus a new request. Then describe the change-request process: the client asks in writing, you quote the extra time or fee, and work starts after approval.

This is the clause that stops scope creep without a fight. **Mistake to avoid:** "unlimited revisions," which is a promise to work on one project until one of you gives up.

### 9. Client responsibilities

Your work depends on the client: content, logins, feedback, approvals, access to people. List what they must provide and by when.

Connect this clause to your timeline. If inputs arrive late, deadlines move by the same amount. **Mistake to avoid:** treating the client's homework as a favor instead of a contract term.

### 10. Cancellation and rescheduling

Say how either side can cancel or reschedule, how much notice is needed ([NUMBER] days), and what is owed for work already done or time already reserved.

For session-based work, add a rescheduling window and what happens with no-shows. **Mistake to avoid:** a cancellation clause that only covers the client, leaving you no clean way out of a bad fit.

### 11. Intellectual property and ownership

This clause decides who owns what you create. Under US copyright law, [work made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf) applies in two situations: work an employee creates as part of regular duties, or certain types of commissioned work created under an express written agreement. In those cases the hiring party is the author and copyright owner.

Otherwise, the creator is ordinarily the author. That's why many freelancers and agencies keep ownership until the final payment clears, then transfer it or grant a license. **Mistake to avoid:** handing over ownership the moment work is delivered, before you're paid.

### 12. Confidentiality

Both sides usually share private information: client lists, strategy, logins, pricing. This clause says what counts as confidential, how it's protected and how long the duty lasts.

Keep it mutual, because you share private details too. **Mistake to avoid:** a one-sided clause that binds you but not the client.

### 13. Limitation of liability and indemnity

A limitation of liability clause caps how much either side can claim if something goes wrong. Many service businesses tie the cap to the fees paid under the contract (left as a blank in the template).

[Indemnity](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/indemnity) means one party agrees to cover certain losses or claims the other faces. In plain English: "if your material gets us sued, you handle it." **This is the clause to have a lawyer word for you**, because the details matter more here than anywhere else.

### 14. Termination and dispute resolution

Explain how either side can end the agreement early: with notice, or right away after a serious [breach of contract](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach_of_contract). Then say what happens to paid work, unpaid work and work in progress.

Finish with how disputes get handled: talk first, then mediation or another method, plus which state's law applies. **Mistake to avoid:** no clause about work in progress, which turns an ending into a second fight.

## Free service contract template (fill in the blanks)

Copy this skeleton, replace every [BRACKETED] item, and delete what doesn't apply. It's a starting point for your lawyer, not a finished legal document.

> **SERVICE AGREEMENT**
>
> 1. **Parties and effective date:** This Service Agreement is between [YOUR LEGAL BUSINESS NAME], [ADDRESS] ("Provider"), and [CLIENT LEGAL NAME], [ADDRESS] ("Client"). It takes effect on [EFFECTIVE DATE].
> 2. **Scope of services:** Provider will perform: [SERVICES, WITH QUANTITIES AND LIMITS]. Not included: [ITEMS OUTSIDE THE SCOPE].
> 3. **Deliverables and acceptance:** Provider will deliver: [DELIVERABLES AND FORMATS]. Client will approve or send specific written feedback within [NUMBER] business days, or the deliverable is treated as accepted.
> 4. **Timeline and milestones:** Start: [DATE]. Milestones: [MILESTONES AND DATES]. End or term: [DATE OR "ONGOING"]. Dates move by the number of days any Client input arrives late.
> 5. **Fees and payment terms:** Client will pay [FLAT FEE / HOURLY RATE / MONTHLY RETAINER] of [AMOUNT]. Provider invoices [SCHEDULE]; invoices are due within [NUMBER] days by [PAYMENT METHODS].
> 6. **Deposit or retainer:** A deposit of [DEPOSIT AMOUNT] is due on signing and reserves Provider's time. It is [REFUNDABLE / NON-REFUNDABLE] and is credited toward [FINAL INVOICE / FIRST MONTH].
> 7. **Late payments and expenses:** If an invoice is unpaid [NUMBER] days after its due date, Provider may pause work until it is paid. Late fee, where permitted by law: [TERMS]. Expenses: [WHICH EXPENSES], billed [AT COST / WITH PRIOR WRITTEN APPROVAL].
> 8. **Revisions and change requests:** The fee includes [NUMBER] revision rounds per deliverable. Provider quotes any change request in writing, and work on it starts only after Client approves the quote in writing.
> 9. **Client responsibilities:** Client will provide [CONTENT / ACCESS / APPROVALS / CONTACT PERSON] by [DATE].
> 10. **Cancellation and rescheduling:** Either party may reschedule with [NUMBER] days' written notice. If Client cancels, Client pays for work completed to date plus [CANCELLATION TERMS].
> 11. **Intellectual property and ownership:** Provider owns all work until full payment is received, then [ASSIGNS OWNERSHIP OF / LICENSES] the final deliverables to Client: [DETAILS]. Provider keeps its pre-existing tools and templates. Portfolio use: [ALLOWED / AFTER LAUNCH / NOT ALLOWED].
> 12. **Confidentiality:** Each party keeps the other's non-public information confidential and uses it only for this Agreement, during it and for [NUMBER] years after it ends.
> 13. **Limitation of liability and indemnity:** [LIABILITY CAP WORDING, E.G. TIED TO THE FEES PAID UNDER THIS AGREEMENT]. [INDEMNITY WORDING, WRITTEN OR REVIEWED BY A LAWYER].
> 14. **Termination and dispute resolution:** Either party may end this Agreement with [NUMBER] days' written notice, or at once if the other seriously breaches it and doesn't fix it within [NUMBER] days of notice. Client pays for all work completed up to the end date. Disputes are first discussed in good faith, then handled by [MEDIATION / OTHER METHOD]. Governing law: [STATE].
>
> **Signatures:** Provider: [NAME], [TITLE], signature ________, date [DATE]. Client: [NAME], [TITLE], signature ________, date [DATE].

## How to adapt the template for your type of service business

The skeleton works across industries, but each type of service business has its own pressure points. Here is where to spend your editing time.

### Marketing agencies

Retainers live or die on the scope clause, so list monthly deliverables with numbers. Add who owns ad accounts, pixels and creative, and who keeps account access if the relationship ends. Define approval rounds per campaign, not per project.

### Consultants and coaches

Count sessions, define how long each one runs, and set a clear rescheduling window. Add no-guarantee-of-results wording: you deliver advice and sessions, not a specific business outcome. Clients respect the honesty more than you'd think.

### Designers and freelancers

Be specific about revision rounds and file formats, including whether editable source files are part of the deal. Add portfolio-use rights so you can show the work later. That one sentence saves a very uncomfortable email.

### Photographers

Shoots bring their own issues: image rights, usage licenses, weather, second shooters and travel. For those, use our [photography contract template](/photography-contract-template/), which covers shoot-specific clauses this general version leaves out.

## How to send and e-sign your service contract

Good news: you don't need a printer. Under the US [ESIGN Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-106publ229) (Public Law 106-229, passed in 2000), a signature or contract can't be denied legal effect just because it's in electronic form.

The bigger question is speed. Clients sign faster when the contract arrives in the same flow as the proposal and the first invoice.

One link, one sitting, done. A contract emailed as an attachment after the proposal tends to sit in an inbox.

That's why many owners send contracts from client management tools instead of a separate document app:

- **HoneyBook** sends proposals, contracts and invoices to clients for e-signature and payment.
- **Dubsado** sends contracts for e-signature as part of its client workflows.
- **Bonsai** sends contracts for e-signature alongside proposals and invoices for freelancers.

2. Send the proposal, contract and deposit invoice together.
3. Once it's signed, hand the client off to your [client onboarding software](/client-onboarding-software/) or welcome sequence, so the momentum carries into the first week.

## Don't mess this up: 6 service contract mistakes

Most contract problems aren't about clever legal wording. They come from small, boring gaps that nobody noticed until money or deadlines were on the line.

**Don't start work before it's signed.** A "we'll sign it later" project has no rules when the first disagreement shows up. Make the signature and deposit the trigger for day one.

**Don't write a vague scope.** If you can't count it, the client can stretch it. Put numbers, formats and limits on every deliverable.

**Don't skip the change-request clause.** Without it, every extra ask turns into a negotiation about fairness. With it, it's just a quote and a yes or no.

**Don't leave out late-payment terms.** A pause-work right and a clear due date fix most late payers without any drama. Check your state's rules before adding a late fee.

**Don't copy a template from another country or industry without adapting it.** Contract rules, notice periods and consumer protections differ by place and trade. A clause that is normal in one setting can be unenforceable in another.

**Don't skip a lawyer's review for high-value work.** A one-time review of your master template costs far less than one dispute. Ask them to look hardest at liability, indemnity and ownership.

## FAQs About the Service Contract Template

### What should a service contract include?

At minimum: the parties and effective date, scope of services, deliverables and acceptance, timeline, fees and payment terms, deposit, late payments and expenses, revisions and change requests, client responsibilities, cancellation, intellectual property, confidentiality, limitation of liability and indemnity, and termination with dispute resolution. That's the 14-clause list in this guide.

### Is a service agreement the same as a service contract?

In everyday business use, yes. Both describe a binding agreement in which one side provides services and the other pays for them. What matters is the content and the signatures, not the label at the top.

### Are e-signed service contracts legally valid?

In the US, the ESIGN Act says a signature or contract can't be denied legal effect solely because it's electronic. Industry or state rules can still add requirements for some documents, so check with a lawyer if your field is regulated.

### Who owns the work under a service contract?

It depends on the contract. Work made for hire applies only to employees' regular work or to certain commissioned works under an express written agreement; otherwise the creator ordinarily owns the copyright. Spell out ownership and any license in the intellectual property clause.

### What is the difference between a proposal and a service contract?

A proposal is the pitch before the client says yes: the idea, the approach and the price. The service contract is the binding set of terms after they say yes. Many businesses send both in one flow so the client can accept and sign at once.

### Do I need a lawyer to use a service contract template?

You can start with a template, but have a lawyer review your final version before you rely on it, especially the liability, indemnity and ownership clauses. One review of a master template you reuse is usually money well spent.
