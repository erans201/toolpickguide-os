It states the client’s goal, your plan, the price, and the timeline, in a way that makes the decision feel safe.</p><p>Best timing: send it after you’ve confirmed fit and scope on a call, and before you start work. It’s also the right move when an inbound lead says “send me a quote”, because the proposal gives the quote a brain and a spine.</p><p>Here’s the clean separation:</p><ul><li><strong>Proposal</strong>: pitch before yes.</li><li><strong>Contract</strong>: binding terms after yes.</li><li><strong>Business plan</strong>: for lenders/investors, not clients. See SBA’s guide to a <a href="https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/write-your-business-plan">business plan</a>.</li></ul><p>A proposal can also function as an <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/offer">offer</a> in contract law, depending on how you write and present it. Cornell’s plain-English idea is: an offer is made in a way that would lead a reasonable person to expect a binding contract from its <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/acceptance">acceptance</a>.</p><p>Set expectations like this: proposals sell clarity and reduce risk; contracts handle legal protection. In many service businesses, you send proposal first, contract second, or you use proposal + acceptance as part of your agreement depending on your process.</p><p>This is general information, not legal advice.</p><h2>The 10 sections of a proposal template that wins clients</h2><p>The principle is simple: make saying yes easy. Clarity, specificity, options, and a frictionless next step.</p><p>Think of a proposal with one price like a menu with one dish. The client can only say yes or leave, and leaving is the path of least resistance.</p><p>The sections below form a reusable proposal template for most service businesses.</p><p>To make this easier to implement, add simple visuals to break up text. A screenshot of your <a href="https://betterproposals.io/blog/pricing-table-mistakes/">pricing table</a>, a simple timeline graphic, or a one-page “deliverables” graphic can do a lot of work for you.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1450101215322-bf5cd27642fc?auto=format&amp;fit=crop&amp;w=1600&amp;q=70" alt="Proposal layout sketch with cover page, scope, timeline, pricing, and sign-off sections"></p><h3>1. Cover page</h3><p>Your cover page is not decoration. It’s the “yes, this is for me” moment.</p><p>Summarize the client’s goal in their words and include one or two measurable outcomes. Then restate what you’re recommending and the expected result, in plain language.</p><p>Keep it skimmable: three to six sentences. No jargon. No origin story.</p><p>Include the basics so it feels official and easy to forward:</p><ul><li>Project name (example: “Website Redesign Proposal for Acme Corp”)</li><li>[Client Name] + main contact</li><li>[Your Business Name] + contact info</li><li>Date</li><li>Optional: version number (helpful when scope changes)</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: leaving out contact details or version control. If they forward it internally and your name is nowhere, you’re making it harder to buy.</p><h3>2. The client's goal (in their words)</h3><p>This is where you demonstrate your attentiveness during the discovery call. Use their language, not yours.</p><p>List the top three problems or goals you discovered, as bullets. Then add a short snapshot of what’s happening today, what’s at stake, and what they’ve already tried.</p><p>Finally, show the cost of inaction without getting dramatic. The point is clarity, not fear.</p><p>Example framing:</p><ul><li>What’s happening now</li><li>What “better” looks like</li><li>What it costs to wait (time, missed revenue, stress, internal churn)</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: generic goals like “improve online presence.” If the goal could be pasted into any proposal, it won’t feel personal.</p><h3>3. Your approach</h3><p>This is the plan, at the “smart friend” level. Not vague, not overly technical.</p><p>Describe deliverables and responsibilities, and separate yours from theirs. Use testable language, meaning the client can point at the page and say “yes, I got that.”</p><p>Also, include boundaries. Scope creep isn’t a personality flaw, it’s what happens when a proposal is blurry.</p><p>Good things to define here:</p><ul><li>Platforms included (and not included)</li><li>Pages, campaigns, or assets included</li><li>Revision limits</li><li>Meeting cadence</li><li>Feedback turnaround expectations</li></ul><p>A well-structured approach can be likened to a <a href="https://www.rock.so/blog/project-charter">project charter</a>, which serves as a roadmap for successful project execution.</p><p>Example (simple): “We’ll audit your current site, propose a new homepage structure, design two concepts, implement the approved design, and QA before launch.”</p><p>Mistake to avoid: being too vague (“we’ll improve your brand”) or too technical (“we’ll refactor your component library”) for the buyer reading it.</p><h3>4. Scope and deliverables</h3><p>Yes, “scope and deliverables” sounds similar to “your approach.” Here’s the difference:</p><ul><li><strong>Approach</strong> explains how you’ll work.</li><li><strong>Scope/deliverables</strong> lists exactly what they get.</li></ul><p>Outline phases like kickoff → research → execution → review → launch. Give a realistic timeline with dependencies, like client feedback, access, assets, approvals.</p><p>Also include your communication rhythm. People buy calm as much as they buy the work.</p><p>Common rhythm language:</p><ul><li>Weekly email update</li><li>One check-in call per week or per milestone</li><li>Typical turnaround time for reviews</li><li>Where communication happens (email, Slack, portal)</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: leaving deliverables implied. “Full service” is not a deliverable.</p><h3>5. Timeline and milestones</h3><p>Convert the project into concrete checkpoints. Clients relax when they can see the road.</p><p>Include milestones that match your phases and note dependencies. If you’re waiting on logins, content, or approvals, say it clearly.</p><p>Also: don’t use “ASAP.” “ASAP” is a guarantee you can’t keep, because it depends on other humans.</p><p>Example structure:</p><ul><li>Week 1: kickoff + access + baseline</li><li>Week 2: research + first draft</li><li>Week 3: review + revisions</li><li>Week 4: launch + handoff</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: no deadlines at all. A proposal without time is a project that never starts.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1506784926709-22f1ec395907?auto=format&amp;fit=crop&amp;w=1600&amp;q=70" alt="Simple calendar and milestone planning board"></p><h3>6. Pricing table</h3><p>This is where most “email quotes” fail. They give one number, with no explanation, so the buyer has to invent the reasoning. That’s like asking them to write your sales story for you.</p><p>Present two to three package options (Good/Better/Best) or tiers by scope. Keep the differences obvious, and keep the quality consistent. You’re changing volume or breadth, not doing “worse work” on the cheap plan.</p><p>Show totals clearly, specify billing model (one-time, monthly retainer, milestone-based), and explain what drives cost in plain language (time, complexity, volume).</p><p>Include:</p><ul><li>Payment schedule</li><li>Light-touch late fee policy</li><li>What happens if scope changes (change request process)</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: hiding the number on the last page like it’s a confession. Put pricing where it’s easy to find, and anchor it to outcomes and deliverables.</p><p>You can build this table in any decent doc tool, but proposal tools like HoneyBook, Dubsado, and Bonsai make acceptance easier. These platforms often allow for online acceptance of proposals which can streamline the process significantly. However, if you're looking for a more comprehensive solution that includes features like e-signatures, it's worth exploring some of the <a href="https://toolpickguide.com/best-doc-signing-tools/">best doc signing tools</a> available.</p><h3>7. Why you: proof and results</h3><p>Keep this tight. The goal is trust, not a biography.</p><p>Add one to three short case snippets in this format:</p><ul><li>Problem</li><li>Work done</li><li>Result (if you have it)</li></ul><p>Then add testimonials that match the client’s industry or goal. If you have a mini portfolio, link it or add a couple screenshots.</p><p>If you don’t have testimonials yet, don’t panic. Use “relevant experience” bullets: similar projects, years in the niche, specific systems you use, or outcomes you’ve helped with, stated honestly.</p><p>Mistake to avoid: overloading this section. Proof should feel like seasoning, not the whole meal.</p><h3>8. Key terms in plain English</h3><p>This section prevents awkward surprises. It’s your “here’s how this goes smoothly” page.</p><p>List assumptions that affect timeline or results:</p><ul><li>Access to accounts</li><li>Approval turnaround</li><li>Brand assets and content availability</li><li>Technical constraints (if relevant)</li></ul><p>Define client responsibilities:</p><ul><li>Single point of contact</li><li>Feedback deadlines</li><li>Providing content/assets</li><li>Attending key meetings</li></ul><p>State how delays affect timeline and launch dates. Be neutral, not threatening.</p><p>If you mention signing, you can keep it plain-English: an electronic signature is a signature in electronic form. Cornell’s explainer on <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/electronic_signature">electronic signature</a> is a good reference point, and the <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-106publ229">ESIGN Act</a> says a signature or contract may not be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic.</p><h3>9. Next steps</h3><p>This is where you stop being “informative” and start being “easy to buy.”</p><p>Include:</p><ul><li>Proposal validity period (example: valid for [X] days)</li><li>Start date window (example: can begin the week of [Date])</li><li>A plain-language change request clause</li><li>Cancellation or pausing policy at a high level (details in contract)</li><li>Ownership/usage basics (who owns final assets; third-party costs billed separately)</li></ul><p>Mistake to avoid: “Let me know your thoughts.” That sentence is the proposal equivalent of leaving your keys in the door.</p><h3>10. Make it obvious what they get, what it costs, and how to start.</p><h3>1) Recap the client’s goal in their words</h3><p>Before you write anything, pull your notes and extract the exact phrases they used. Mirror those on the cover page and in the goals section.</p><p>People trust what feels familiar. If your proposal sounds like them, it feels safer.</p><h3>2) Choose a simple approach with a beginning, middle, and end</h3><p>Don’t overcomplicate the method. Clear phases beat fancy frameworks.</p><p>If you can’t explain your approach in a few bullets, the client won’t be able to sell it internally.</p><h3>3) Write scope like a checklist, not a paragraph</h3><p>Scope is where ambiguity goes to die.
