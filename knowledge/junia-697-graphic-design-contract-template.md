**A graphic design contract template** is what stands between you and round eleven of "just one more small tweak."

Without a written agreement, the same problems keep showing up: endless revisions, a client who vanishes right after approving the logo, an argument over who owns which files, or a project cancelled halfway with nothing paid.

This guide gives you the 12 clauses that cover the messy parts, a copy-ready template outline you can paste into a doc, and a simple way to get it signed online.

## Quick answer: what a graphic design contract needs

1. **Parties and project overview:** who the deal is between and what you're making.
2. **Scope of work:** what's included and, just as important, what isn't.
3. **Deliverables and file formats:** the exact final files the client gets.
4. **Timeline and client approvals:** dates that move when feedback is late.
5. **Fees and payment schedule:** what gets billed when, with due dates.
6. **Deposit before work starts:** no deposit, no design.
7. **Revision rounds and change requests:** a fixed number of rounds, then billed changes.
8. **Copyright transfer on final payment:** rights move only once you're paid in full.
9. **Usage rights and licensing:** license or assignment, and where the work may be used.
10. **Source files and preliminary concepts:** working files are priced separately or stay with you.
11. **Portfolio and credit rights:** your right to show the finished work.
12. **Cancellation, kill fee and termination:** what gets paid if the project stops early.

## Why designers need their own contract (not a generic one)

At its core, [a contract](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/contract) is an agreement between parties that creates mutual obligations enforceable by law. That's the whole point: both sides know what they owe each other, in writing.

A generic agreement usually covers the obvious stuff: scope, payment, maybe a start date. That's fine for plenty of services, but design work has its own danger zones.

Design projects need extra clarity on revision rounds, who owns the copyright, whether the client gets the working files, and whether you can show the finished work in your portfolio. These are the spots where email threads go to die.

Here's what that ambiguity looks like in real life:

- The logo gets approved, then the final invoice goes unpaid, and now you're debating whether the client can use it anyway.
- The client assumes they're getting editable files, but you priced a finished set of deliverables, not your whole working kitchen.
- "Unlimited revisions" gets read two different ways, like an all-you-can-eat buffet where you're also the cook.

If you offer other kinds of work besides design, use a general [service contract template](https://toolpickguide.com/service-contract-template/) for those jobs and keep your design agreement separate.

Also worth reading: AIGA, the professional association for design, publishes a [Standard Form of Agreement for Design Services](https://www.aiga.org/sites/default/files/2021-04/standard-form-of-agreement-for-design-services.pdf). It isn't a fill-in-the-blank form. AIGA built it as terms and conditions you attach to your own project proposal, and the signed proposal plus those terms form the agreement.

## The 12 clauses every graphic design contract template needs

Treat this as a working outline, not a substitute for a lawyer's review. Keep the language plain and get your final version reviewed before you use it on real projects.

You can copy the outline into Google Docs, Notion or Word. Use bracketed fields like [CLIENT LEGAL NAME], [PROJECT NAME] and [PROJECT FEE], then swap in real values for each project.

Make it reusable by keeping one master contract and adding a per-project statement of work as an exhibit. Think of it as your base layer, with the project-specific toppings added each time.

### 1. Parties and project overview

This clause names who the agreement is between: [DESIGNER OR STUDIO LEGAL NAME] and [CLIENT LEGAL NAME], plus contact details and an effective date. It's boring until you need it, and then it's the most important paragraph you wrote all year.

Add a short summary of what you're hired to do, like "design a brand identity system" or "create social templates for a campaign." Keep it factual, not salesy.

Mistake to avoid: leaving out the client's legal business name, or not naming who can approve work. "My point of contact is Jenna" is not the same as "Jenna can approve deliverables and timelines."

### 2. Scope of work

Spell out what's included and what's not. If it's "logo plus basic usage guidelines," say that, and also say "not included: full brand guidelines, naming, packaging, website design," or whatever applies.

AIGA's guidance helps here: state the number of creative directions you'll present and the number of revisions included. When scope is vague, scope creep doesn't feel like creep. It feels like "quick favors."

Mistake to avoid: a scope that reads "designer will design a logo package." That's an invitation for the client to define "package" as "everything I can think of before Friday."

### 3. Deliverables and file formats

List the exact final files the client receives, and their formats. Don't say "logo package" like it's a known ingredient. Name the ingredients.

Also say how and when the files are delivered, for example via [DELIVERY METHOD] within [NUMBER] days of final approval and final payment. That keeps "where are the files?" emails out of your inbox late at night.

Mistake to avoid: skipping the file list. If the client expected print-ready files and you delivered web-only formats, you've got a problem one bullet list would have prevented.

### 4. Timeline and client approvals

Put the timeline in writing, but be honest about what controls it: the client's feedback and approvals. Most design timelines are really "designer time plus client time," and client time is the wild card.

State what happens when feedback is late, such as the schedule moving by the length of the delay. This is less about punishment and more about reality.

Mistake to avoid: promising fixed dates that ignore client delays. That's how you end up working nights because someone took weeks to reply "looks good."

### 5. Fees and payment schedule

State the project fee as [PROJECT FEE]. Then set out when payments are due, by milestone or by date, and give every invoice a real due date.

This is where you define the money rhythm: what gets billed when, and what "due" means. If you don't define it, the client will default to "whenever accounting gets to it."

Mistake to avoid: invoices with no due date. "Net whenever" is not a payment term.

### 6. Deposit before work starts

Make it plain: work starts only after the agreement is signed and the deposit of [DEPOSIT AMOUNT] is paid. Not "sent," not "processing," not "my bookkeeper is out."

Keep the deposit wording the same across your proposal, invoice and contract. Consistency prevents the "I thought the deposit was optional" conversation.

Mistake to avoid: starting design before the deposit clears. That's like spotting a stranger at the gym with a fully loaded barbell. Nice gesture, bad plan.

### 7. Revision rounds and change requests

Include a fixed number of revision rounds: [NUMBER] rounds, with extra work billed at [HOURLY RATE]. Define what counts as one round in your process, so one email with a long list of bullets doesn't become a philosophical debate.

Require changes in direction to go through a written change request. That can be as simple as an email both sides confirm, but it needs to exist.

Mistake to avoid: promising unlimited revisions. Unlimited revisions is like an all-you-can-eat buffet where you're also the cook, the server and the dishwasher.

### 8. Copyright transfer on final payment

By default, the creator is ordinarily the author and owns the copyright. A work is [made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf) only in two situations: it was created by an employee as part of regular duties, or it's a certain type of commissioned work covered by an express written agreement.

A transfer of copyright ownership also has to be [in writing and signed](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204) by the owner of the rights (or the owner's authorized agent). The AIGA terms make the client's rights conditional on payment in full, and designers commonly transfer or license the rights only after final payment.

Mistake to avoid: handing over ownership before the final invoice is paid. If you do, you lose your biggest leverage and you're hoping politeness will replace process.

### 9. Usage rights and licensing

Be clear about the difference between a license and an assignment. A [license](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/license) is permission to use the work; with an assignment, ownership changes hands.

AIGA's form offers four options: a license for limited usage, an exclusive license, an assignment of rights, or work made for hire. Your contract should say which one applies, and where and how the client may use the work.

Mistake to avoid: leaving usage undefined. A vague "you can use it" can be read as "we can do anything with it forever, in every medium, including things you never priced."

### 10. Source files and preliminary concepts

AIGA's framing is simple: the client normally gets rights in the final works, not in sketches, rejected concepts or working files, unless the deal is work made for hire. Say this in plain language.

If the client wants the editable source files, put that in the contract and price it. Source files aren't just files; they're your process, your structure and your future flexibility.

Mistake to avoid: handing over source files you never priced. That's like giving away the kitchen because someone liked the meal.

### 11. Portfolio and credit rights

State your right to show the finished work in your portfolio and client list. If you want a credit line (like "designed by"), this is where it goes.

Add a basic confidentiality carve-out too: you won't publicly show anything that contains the client's confidential information. That's how the AIGA form handles it.

Mistake to avoid: leaving portfolio rights out, then asking awkward permission later to show work you created.

### 12. Cancellation, kill fee and termination

Define what happens if the project is cancelled. Under the AIGA approach, final billing can be time and materials up to that date, a lump-sum cancellation fee often called a "kill fee," or both. Write it as [KILL FEE AMOUNT].

Say what the client receives on cancellation, typically the latest approved version, and that preliminary art stays with the designer. If one side doesn't perform at all, that's a [breach of contract](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach_of_contract), and your termination clause should say what happens next.

Mistake to avoid: having no exit plan. Projects end early all the time, and "we'll figure it out" is not a policy.

Paste this into a doc and replace every [BRACKETED] field. The 12 sections follow the clauses above, in the same order.

**1. Parties and project overview**

- Effective date: [EFFECTIVE DATE]
- Designer/studio: [DESIGNER LEGAL NAME], [BUSINESS TYPE], [ADDRESS], [EMAIL], [PHONE]
- Client: [CLIENT LEGAL NAME], [BUSINESS TYPE], [ADDRESS], [EMAIL], [PHONE]
- Project: [PROJECT NAME] · Summary: [SHORT DESCRIPTION OF WHAT IS BEING DESIGNED AND WHY]
- Client approver: [NAME AND TITLE OF THE PERSON WHO CAN APPROVE WORK]

**2. Scope of work**

- Included services: [SERVICE 1], [SERVICE 2], [SERVICE 3]
- Creative directions presented: [NUMBER]
- Not included: [EXCLUSION 1], [EXCLUSION 2], [EXCLUSION 3]

**3. Deliverables and file formats**

- Final files: [FILE 1 AND FORMAT], [FILE 2 AND FORMAT], [FILE 3 AND FORMAT]
- Delivery: via [DELIVERY METHOD] within [NUMBER] days of final approval and final payment.

**4. Timeline and client approvals**

- Kickoff: [DATE] · First presentation: [DATE] · Final approval target: [DATE]
- Client provides [CONTENT AND ASSETS NEEDED] and gives feedback within [NUMBER] business days of each delivery.
- If feedback or approvals are late, the schedule moves by the length of the delay.

**5. Fees and payment schedule**

- Project fee: [PROJECT FEE]
- Payments: [MILESTONE 1]: [AMOUNT] due [DATE] · [MILESTONE 2]: [AMOUNT] due [DATE] · [MILESTONE 3]: [AMOUNT] due [DATE]
- Each invoice is due on the date shown on it. Designer may pause work while an invoice is overdue.
- Third-party costs (printing, stock assets, fonts): [WHO PAYS, AND WHETHER APPROVAL IS NEEDED FIRST]

**6. Deposit before work starts**

- Work begins after this agreement is signed and the deposit of [DEPOSIT AMOUNT] is paid.

**7. Revision rounds and change requests**

- Included: [NUMBER] revision rounds. One round means: [DEFINITION, FOR EXAMPLE ONE COMBINED SET OF FEEDBACK ON ONE DELIVERABLE].
- Extra rounds and new work are billed at [HOURLY RATE]. Changes in direction need a written change request signed off by both sides.

**8. Copyright transfer on final payment**

- Designer owns the copyright in all work until payment in full.
- After payment in full of all amounts due, Designer transfers to Client: [RIGHTS TRANSFERRED]. This transfer is made in this signed writing.
- Work made for hire (only if agreed and if it qualifies): [WORK MADE FOR HIRE LANGUAGE, OR "NOT APPLICABLE"]

**9. Usage rights and licensing**

- Rights granted (choose one): [LICENSE FOR LIMITED USAGE / EXCLUSIVE LICENSE / ASSIGNMENT OF RIGHTS / WORK MADE FOR HIRE]
- Permitted media, territory and term: [MEDIA], [TERRITORY], [TERM]

**10. Source files and preliminary concepts**

- Editable source files: [INCLUDED, WITH LIST / NOT INCLUDED]. If added later, they are priced at [SOURCE FILE FEE].
- Sketches, rejected concepts and working files remain with Designer.

**11. Portfolio and credit rights**

- Designer may show the final work in its portfolio and list Client as a client.
- Designer will not publicly show work containing Client's confidential information.
- Credit line (optional): [CREDIT LINE] in [LOCATIONS]

**12. Cancellation, kill fee and termination**

- Either party may end this agreement with [NUMBER] days' written notice.
- On cancellation, Client pays [TIME AND MATERIALS TO DATE / KILL FEE OF [KILL FEE AMOUNT] / BOTH].
- After payment, Client receives the latest approved version; Designer keeps all preliminary art.

**Entire agreement:** This agreement and any attached statement of work are the whole agreement and replace earlier discussions.

**Signatures**

- Designer/studio: Name [NAME] · Business [BUSINESS NAME] · Signature ________ · Date [DATE]
- Client: Name [NAME] · Business [CLIENT LEGAL NAME] · Signature ________ · Date [DATE]

## Freelance designer vs design agency: what changes

Agencies have an extra layer: if you hire freelancers or subcontractors, get IP rights from them that match what you're promising the client. AIGA flags this point, and it's easy to miss when you're staffing up fast.

Agencies also tend to add account contacts, approval chains and sometimes retainer language. More people touching the project means more places for "I thought you meant…" to sneak in.

Freelancers can keep it lean, especially when they work solo and the client approver is clear. If you're working under an agency's contract, make sure your own terms don't conflict with what the agency promised its client.

## How to send and e-sign your graphic design contract

In the US, the [ESIGN Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-106publ229) says a signature or contract can't be denied legal effect just because it's electronic. So you can send agreements and collect signatures without printing, scanning or mailing anything.

Send the contract right after the proposal, not a week later when everyone has moved on. Pair it with your [proposal template](https://toolpickguide.com/proposal-template/) and the deposit invoice, so the client can approve, sign and pay in one sitting.

Client management tools like **Bonsai**, **HoneyBook** and **Dubsado** can send your contract for e-signature as part of the same flow as the proposal and invoice. ## Don't mess this up: 6 graphic design contract mistakes

**Don't start designing before it's signed and the deposit is paid.** An email "sounds good" is not a signed agreement. Starting early tells the client your process is negotiable.

**Don't promise unlimited revisions.** It sounds generous, but it hands the client an open-ended claim on your time. Set a number of rounds and a rate for anything beyond them.

**Don't hand over source files you never priced.** Editable files change the value of what you're selling. If the client wants full access, price it and write it into the contract.

**Don't transfer copyright before final payment.** Tie the transfer or license to payment in full. Otherwise you're chasing an invoice with no leverage left.

**Don't forget the kill fee.** Projects get paused, budgets get cut and new decision-makers appear. A kill fee is your safety net when that happens.

**Don't copy a contract from another country or skip a lawyer on big projects.** Laws vary, and large engagements carry real risk. Use a template as your starting point, then have a lawyer review it.

## FAQs (Frequently Asked Questions)

### What is a graphic design contract template and why do I need one?

A graphic design contract template is a reusable agreement between a designer and a client. It heads off endless revisions, unpaid invoices, fights over file ownership and messy cancellations by setting expectations, scope, payment terms and rights in writing.

### What clauses should every graphic design contract include?

Cover these 12: parties and project overview; scope of work; deliverables and file formats; timeline and client approvals; fees and payment schedule; deposit; revision rounds and change requests; copyright transfer on final payment; usage rights and licensing; source files and preliminary concepts; portfolio and credit rights; and cancellation, kill fee and termination.

### How can I avoid scope creep in my design projects?

Say exactly what's included and what's excluded, for example "logo plus basic usage guidelines" and "no full brand guidelines or website design." Also state how many creative directions and revision rounds are included, and send changes in direction through a written change request.

### Why can't I just use a generic contract for design work?

Generic agreements usually cover scope, payment and start dates. Design work adds revision limits, copyright, source files and portfolio rights, and those need to be spelled out to avoid misunderstandings.

### Who owns the copyright in a design project?

By default, the creator is ordinarily the author and owner. A copyright transfer has to be in writing and signed by the owner, so your contract should say what transfers and when, usually after payment in full.

### Can I use a fill-in-the-blank template for every client?

Yes. Keep one master contract with bracketed fields, add a statement of work for each project, and have a lawyer review your final version before you rely on it.
